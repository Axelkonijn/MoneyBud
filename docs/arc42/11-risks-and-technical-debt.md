# 11. Risks and Technical Debt

**What belongs here:** Known technical risks and accumulated debt, stated plainly, ideally with
what could be done about each.

This section is only worth anything if it is honest. A risk section listing no real risks tells the
reader the document isn't maintained.

---

| Risk / debt | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Manual entry and accurate net worth pull against each other.** An accurate net worth requires every transaction to be recorded; requiring that is exactly the friction that makes people abandon budgeting apps. The app cannot detect a forgotten transaction, because nothing outside it knows what the truth is | High — this is the most likely cause of the product quietly becoming useless | Not solved. Options include letting balances be corrected by hand, or reconciling entered balances against calculated ones and reporting the difference. Bank import would largely remove it, but conflicts with local operation |
| **Two mechanisms will write the same account balance.** Once accounts exist, an *account-backed* category ([§12](12-glossary.md)) makes a balance move as a **side effect of a planning action**: assigning to Savings transfers real money into its backing account, and the end-of-period sweep does the same unprompted. Both movements now have a designated **source** as well — the *pool account* ([§12](12-glossary.md)) — so one everyday account is written automatically by every assignment to a backed category and by every sweep, which makes it the single balance most exposed to this. Meanwhile round 2 settles that the user can also edit a balance freely by hand. So a balance is authored from two directions — one automatic, one manual — and, as the row above records, nothing outside MoneyBud knows which is right. The failure is quiet: the user tops up savings in the bank app, MoneyBud transfers the same money again on assignment, and the balance is double-counted with nothing to contradict it. **A third route reaches a wrong balance from the other side.** An expense whose category is unbacked now *defaults* to the pool account rather than asking ([§12](12-glossary.md)), so an expense paid in **cash** and entered without overriding the account is deducted from the wrong place: the pool account reads low by money that never left it and the cash account reads high by money that did, and nothing outside MoneyBud will say so. Cash is the likely case precisely because nothing about it looks different at entry. This one is **known and accepted** — it was argued out before the default was taken, the override exists, and the decision saved on every expense was judged worth it ([§12](12-glossary.md), quality goal 2) — so it belongs here as a recognised cost, not as an unnoticed hole | Medium, and **latent** — not in the first increment, which has no accounts, so nothing can go wrong yet. Becomes high the moment backing is built, because a wrong balance corrupts net worth, which is one of the two questions the app exists to answer ([§1.1](01-introduction-and-goals.md)). A hand edit also makes the two halves of the model disagree openly: *Accumulated* ([§12](12-glossary.md)) is computed purely from assignments and expenses, so it does not follow an edited backing account, and because backing is many-to-many the difference cannot be attributed to any one category | Not solved, and deliberately not solved now. Decide it **before** accounts are built rather than after: this is exactly the kind of thing that is expensive to discover late, because the choice shapes how balances are stored. The options to weigh are whether a hand-edit is an overwrite or an adjustment entry, whether a balance is stored or derived from its transactions, and whether MoneyBud shows entered and calculated balances side by side rather than picking one. The expense default adds no separate mitigation and needs none: if balances end up **derived from their transactions**, a mis-defaulted account is one entry to re-point rather than a balance to unpick, which is a point in that option's favour. Making the account easy to change after the fact matters more here than any warning at entry would, since the whole point of the default is that nothing at entry knows it is wrong |
| **Single stakeholder, who is also the only user and only developer.** No external party will notice if the product solves the wrong problem | Medium — a wrong direction can run a long way before anything contradicts it | Stakeholder input is recorded verbatim in `docs/stakeholder/` so that later reasoning can be checked against what was actually said, not against what was remembered |
| **Money representation only partly decided** (see [§8.2](08-crosscutting-concepts.md)). Settled: amounts are always a whole number of cents, refused at entry if finer, so nothing in MoneyBud ever rounds. Still open: how amounts are represented in code and how they are stored, the sign convention for inflow versus outflow, and whether the model carries a currency field despite being single-currency | Medium — smaller than it was. The whole-cents rule removes rounding as a source of error, and it constrains the remaining choices rather than depending on them. What is left is still expensive to change once real data exists | Decide the rest before the first money-handling code is written. Low urgency while the first increment is a throwaway demo. Separately, the whole-cents rule holds only while every amount is typed in rather than computed — §8.2 lists what would reopen it |
| **Desktop-first serves the eventual usage pattern badly.** The deployment form is now decided — desktop, [ADR 0002](../decisions/0002-desktop-application-first.md) — which closes the previous "undecided" risk and replaces it with this one. Expenses are entered while out of the house, so mobile is where entry actually happens, and entry friction is what abandons budgeting apps (quality goal 2). A desktop app is the wrong shape for that | Medium — low while the first version is a throwaway demo, because the demo exists to be reacted to rather than lived in. Rises as soon as the stakeholder starts using it in earnest | Accepted knowingly, with the reasoning in ADR 0002. Mobile and desktop/mobile sync stay deferred, not rejected ([§3.2](03-context-and-scope.md)). Keeping scenarios declarative (`features/README.md`) is what lets the specification survive being pointed at a different UI. Reopen the ADR when the demo stops being a demo |
| **The "location" dimension is not in the first increment**, and end-of-period money therefore has nowhere real to go. Transactions recorded in the demo have a category but no account. The knock-on: both *Leftover* and still-*Unassigned* money are supposed to be swept into a chosen destination category at a period end, and [§12](12-glossary.md) now requires that destination to be **account-backed** — precisely so that swept money really lands somewhere. With no accounts there can be no backed category, so there is no valid destination and the sweep cannot run at all; in practice that money vanishes at the boundary | Low, while the data is throwaway. Would become high if the demo quietly turns into the real thing — directing what is left over somewhere useful is a core capability ([§1.1](01-introduction-and-goals.md)), and an account is what it needs to point at | Accepted deliberately, and the vanishing money accepted explicitly by the stakeholder on the grounds that the first version is a demo to react to rather than live in ([§1.1](01-introduction-and-goals.md)) and its data is disposable. It stops being acceptable the moment accounts arrive. Revisit then, and in any case before any data is kept that the stakeholder would mind losing |
